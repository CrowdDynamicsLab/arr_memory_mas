\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organize the findings around three claims. First, boundary-marker survival $\sigma$ and operational-fact survival $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$ \emph{decouple} at the handoff level under compression: they are nearly uncorrelated, and a substantial fraction of handoffs preserve operational content while stripping boundary metadata. Second, marker \emph{strength} (not mere presence) causally determines whether forwarded sensitive content becomes audience-facing leakage --- vague hedges fail almost as badly as no marker, while explicit constraints reduce leakage by an order of magnitude. Third, we observe that prompt-only mitigation and deterministic phrase redaction each address only part of the failure, with paraphrase- and category-level leakage persisting after exact-string redaction, while a gold-derived typed allowlist probe suppresses leakage entirely as an upper bound.~\Cref{tab:results_overview} gives the scale and headline result of each experiment used to support these claims.

\subsection{Boundary Survival Decouples from Operational Survival under Compression (RQ1)}
\label{sec:results_rq1}

\paragraph{Decoupling at the handoff level.}
E1 sweeps the 180-prompt stratified subset on both models. Mean-level statistics are consistent with our central claim (\Cref{tab:e1_baseline}): GPT $\sigma = 0.954$ vs.\ $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}} = 0.994$; DeepSeek $\sigma = 0.880$ vs.\ $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}} = 0.980$. The stronger evidence is at the \emph{individual handoff level}: across 180 handoffs per model, $\sigma$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$ are nearly uncorrelated (Pearson $r = -0.042$ on GPT, $r = 0.086$ on DeepSeek; Spearman $\rho = -0.020$ and $0.131$). The low-$\sigma$ / high-$\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$ quadrant ($\sigma < 0.7$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}} > 0.9$) is populated by 7/180 GPT handoffs and 26/180 DeepSeek handoffs --- concrete examples of operational content surviving intact while boundary metadata is silently stripped. Because $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$ stays near ceiling regardless of where $\sigma$ lands, the failure is not a generic compression-loss effect; it is selective. Full per-handoff $(\sigma, \sigma_{\mathrm{op}})$ data is released alongside the paper.

\paragraph{Compression makes the gap large.}
Modern agent frameworks routinely auto-compress upstream context: LangChain's \texttt{ConversationSummaryMemory}~\citep{mavroudis2024langchain}, AutoGen's compressible message history~\citep{wu2024autogen}, and similar mechanisms summarize when conversation length exceeds a budget. To measure how summary collapse behaves under short memory-entry summarization budgets of the kind used in production-style agent frameworks, E2 adds two hard-budget conditions: 40 words and 25 words / one sentence.~\Cref{tab:e2_stress} shows that under the 25-word budget, $\sigma$ drops sharply while $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$ stays near ceiling on both models. The selective-fragility signature is not an artifact of GPT-5-mini's summarization style; it replicates on a second model family with a different training pipeline.

\subsection{Marker Strength Causally Controls Downstream Leakage (RQ2)}
\label{sec:results_rq2}

E1 reveals many context dumps: sensitive facts forwarded inside internal handoff artifacts, often in fields labeled as local context or content not to forward. The next set of experiments tests whether such dumped content becomes final audience-facing leakage and what causally controls the answer. We construct three handoff variants matched on operational content --- \texttt{no\_dump\_marker} (operational fact plus explicit boundary rule, no sensitive content), \texttt{dump\_with\_marker} (operational fact plus sensitive content plus explicit marker), and \texttt{dump\_no\_marker} (same as the second but with the marker removed) --- and pass each to a downstream agent under four pressure conditions (E3, 144 prompts per model). Because operational content is held constant across variants within this controlled design, any difference in final leakage is attributable to marker presence and not to summarization style, scenario content, or pressure phrasing. E4 adds a fourth variant, \texttt{dump\_weak\_marker}, in which the sensitive content is forwarded with vague privacy language such as ``use discretion.'' E8 extends this into a continuous dose-response by adding two intermediate marker-strength levels (soft suggestion, explicit constraint) between vague language and a hard imperative.

\paragraph{Marker presence: yes/no.}
\Cref{tab:e3e4_leakage} reports final-leakage counts for the four E3/E4 variants. The no-dump variant produces 0/48 leaks on both models, confirming that data minimization is the safest choice when feasible. The dump-with-marker variant keeps leakage low (2/48 GPT, 1/48 DeepSeek). Removing the marker drives leakage to dominate the output (48/48 GPT, 37/48 DeepSeek). Weak markers behave almost identically to no marker (44/48 GPT, 38/48 DeepSeek). Per-pressure breakdowns (\Cref{app:e3,app:e4}) show that the weak-marker failure is uniform across pressures.

\paragraph{Marker strength: dose-response.}
E8 extends the endpoints into a five-level gradient: \texttt{L0\_no\_marker}, \texttt{L1\_vague\_hedge} (``use discretion''), \texttt{L2\_soft\_suggestion} (``please consider not disclosing''), \texttt{L3\_constraint} (``X is confidential''), and \texttt{L4\_imperative} (``do not disclose X''). All other content is held constant.~\Cref{fig:marker_gradient} and~\Cref{tab:marker_gradient} report leakage rates. The curve is sharply non-linear: vague hedges still leak in 35/48 GPT cases and 24/48 DeepSeek cases, while a soft suggestion drops GPT leakage to 6/48 and DeepSeek to 5/48. Explicit constraints and hard imperatives are nearly equivalent. The transition between L1 (vague) and L2 (soft suggestion) is the load-bearing one --- protection comes from the marker being readable as an operational instruction, not from the use of privacy-sounding language. The L1-to-L2 drop is significant under Fisher's exact test for both models (GPT: $p = 2.13\times10^{-9}$; DeepSeek: $p = 4.02\times10^{-5}$). The E3 \texttt{dump\_with\_marker} vs.\ \texttt{dump\_no\_marker} contrast is likewise significant (GPT: $p = 1.61\times10^{-12}$; DeepSeek: $p = 1.98\times10^{-13}$).

\paragraph{What this looks like in practice.}
In a scheduling scenario, the upstream boundary instructs only that Alex is unavailable, with the explicit caveat not to mention an immigration lawyer appointment. Under a weak-marker handoff (``use discretion''), the downstream output states that Alex is unavailable ``due to a prior appointment with an immigration lawyer'' --- the operational fact (unavailability) and the sensitive context (lawyer appointment) both survive, but the licensing rule does not. This is the downstream surface of boundary collapse: a sensitive fact survives, but the rule governing its use is too weak or underspecified to constrain disclosure.

\paragraph{Boundary representation, not handoff topology.}
A natural objection is that the failures so far might be artifacts of the multi-agent handoff step rather than of boundary representation. E5 controls for this by removing the handoff entirely: we deliver the same 12 scenarios directly to a single agent under \texttt{direct\_full\_marker}, \texttt{direct\_weak\_marker}, and \texttt{direct\_no\_marker} and the same four pressures.~\Cref{tab:e5_leakage} shows that raw transcript access with natural-language privacy language is not automatically safe: with a full marker, GPT still leaks in 20/48 cells and DeepSeek in 11/48; weak and absent markers raise the rates substantially. Notably, the direct-full-marker condition (20/48 on GPT) is \emph{more} leaky than the operationalized \texttt{dump\_with\_marker} handoff (2/48 on GPT) from E3, even though the latter routes information through an intermediate agent. The load-bearing axis is whether the boundary is represented as an operational constraint or as natural-language sentiment, not whether the system has multiple agents.

\subsection{Observations on Enforcement (RQ3)}
\label{sec:results_rq3}

The preceding results identify what fails. This subsection records observations about what does not yet fix it, plus one positive existence probe. We position E6 and E7 as observations, not as method contributions: the paper's argument does not depend on a successful mitigation.

\paragraph{Prompt-only mitigation does not stop downstream leakage.}
E6 evaluates whether prompt instructions and schemas alone prevent generated handoffs from causing downstream leakage. We generate 72 handoffs across six prompt-only conditions and run each under four downstream pressures (288 prompts per model). Overall leakage is 58/288 (20\%) on GPT and 42/288 (15\%) on DeepSeek. The nominally safe structured schema is not a reliable mitigation (\Cref{tab:e6_leakage}): \texttt{structured\_schema\_safe} leaks 11/48 on GPT and 6/48 on DeepSeek. Task success remains high (0.997 GPT, 0.986 DeepSeek), so the failure is not driven by refusal. The observation is that prompts and schemas \emph{describe} the boundary; what fails is that nothing \emph{enforces} it.

\paragraph{Phrase-level enforcement leaves semantic leakage.}

The redactor modified 46/72 generated handoffs with 145 total phrase replacements. Exact-phrase leakage drops to 0/288 on both models, and task success is essentially unchanged (0.998 GPT, 0.997 DeepSeek). Representative residuals: an output that omits ``lost their job'' but refers to ``employment status or related details''; an output that removes the literal accommodation-paperwork phrase but still alludes to a temporary schedule accommodation and paperwork; an output that names the redacted material as family-location context. \paragraph{Gold-derived operationalized boundaries eliminate leakage in an existence probe.}
The two observations above show that describing the boundary in prose (E6) and removing forbidden strings (E7) each address only part of the failure. As a positive existence probe, E9 supplies downstream agents with a gold-derived typed handoff in which every fact carries an explicit \texttt{allowed\_audiences} field: operational facts list the downstream audience, sensitive facts have empty allowlists, and the agent is instructed to mention a fact only when the audience appears in its allowlist. Under this condition, final leakage drops to 0/48 on both \texttt{gpt-5-mini} and \texttt{deepseek-r1:32b}. Compared with the prompt-only E6 baseline, this reduction is significant under Fisher's exact test on both models (GPT-5-mini: 0/48 vs.\ 58/288, $p = 1.04\times10^{-4}$; DeepSeek-R1-32B: 0/48 vs.\ 42/288, $p = 1.58\times10^{-3}$). Because the allowlists are hand-constructed from gold scenario annotations rather than produced by a summarizer agent, E9 should be read as an upper-bound existence probe rather than an end-to-end mitigation. The result supports the load-bearing axis identified in E5: when boundary metadata is encoded as an operational access-control constraint, downstream behavior changes; whether generated handoffs can reliably produce and preserve such structure remains an open question. To test whether this effect comes from typed structure itself or from the correctness of the boundary field, we additionally run a corrupted-allowlist stress test on both model families (\Cref{app:e9_corrupted}). In this separate stress-test run, clean allowlists produce near-zero leakage (0/48 on GPT-5-mini; 1/48 on DeepSeek-R1-32B), while corrupted variants substantially restore leakage; in particular, negation-flip corruption leaks 44/48 GPT and 38/48 DeepSeek, close to the no-marker controls of 43/48 and 39/48. This supports the interpretation that the load-bearing factor is not schema formatting alone, but \emph{correct, machine-readable} boundary operationalization.

Taken together, these observations frame the open challenge: prose descriptions and exact-string redaction are insufficient, while a hand-constructed typed access-control schema is sufficient to eliminate leakage on this testbed. What remains is engineering the bridge --- generating, validating, or enforcing such typed schemas automatically from upstream interactions.

\paragraph{Why these results matter.}
The three claims compose into a single design principle for multi-agent coordination. RQ1 shows that the failure is mechanism-specific: handoff compression does not damage downstream agents' ability to act on operational content, only their ability to act on the rules that govern that content. RQ2 shows that the failure is sensitive to representation: marker language that reads as advice (``use discretion'') is treated as advice and may be ignored; marker language that reads as an operational constraint is treated as a constraint and is honored at order-of-magnitude lower leakage rates. RQ3 shows that the standard fixes practitioners reach for first --- adding more prose instructions, redacting forbidden strings --- each address only part of the failure surface; an upper-bound probe further shows that a correctly-extracted typed access-control representation can close the gap entirely. The take-away for designers of multi-agent systems is that privacy guardrails embedded in natural-language prose are likely to degrade with compression, and that the more robust integration point is a typed channel that downstream agents can act on as a constraint rather than read as a suggestion.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:results_summary}

Across two model families, the picture is consistent. Boundary-marker survival decouples from operational-fact survival at the individual-handoff level, and the gap widens monotonically under compression --- pushing into aggressive production-style memory-compression budgets. When sensitive content is forwarded, what determines whether it becomes audience-facing leakage is not the presence of privacy language but the \emph{explicitness} of the boundary rule: vague hedges fail nearly as often as no marker, while explicit constraints drop leakage by an order of magnitude on both models. Raw single-agent transcript access does not by itself avoid this failure mode --- what matters is whether the boundary is represented as an operational constraint, not whether the system has multiple agents. Prompt-only mitigation and exact-string redaction each address only part of the failure; semantic boundary enforcement remains the open challenge. These experiments are designed for causal identification rather than prevalence estimation: they show that boundary metadata can be selectively stripped under handoff compression and that boundary representation controls downstream leakage in matched variants, but they do not estimate the frequency of summary collapse in deployed systems.
