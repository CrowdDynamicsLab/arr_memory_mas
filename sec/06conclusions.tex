\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We have characterized a structural source of privacy leakage in multi-agent LLM systems: handoff compression preferentially preserves operational facts while stripping the boundary metadata that licenses their use. The two measures decouple at the individual handoff level on both {GPT-5-mini} and     DeepSeek-R1-32B (Pearson $r$ near zero); the gap widens under hard compression, and operational survival stays near ceiling even under aggressive 25-word memory-compression budgets. Through matched-variant downstream tests we showed that the load-bearing axis is not the presence of privacy language but its \emph{explicitness}: vague hedges like ``use discretion'' remain high-leakage ($73\%$ on GPT-5-mini, $50\%$ on DeepSeek in the E8 dose-response), and the matched E4 contrast shows that with paraphrase-aware audit they fail almost as often as no marker at all; explicit constraints, by contrast, reduce leakage to under $15\%$ across all three tested models. A no-handoff control further showed that what protects sensitive content is whether the boundary is represented as an operational rule, not whether the system has multiple agents.
These findings reframe what it would take to make multi-agent coordination safe: not better prose summaries, not politer privacy instructions, and not exact-string redaction --- each of which our experiments showed to be insufficient in different ways --- but \emph{operationalized boundaries} that downstream agents can act on as constraints rather than read as suggestions. 
A gold-derived upper-bound probe with a typed audience-allowlist schema reduces leakage to zero on the two primary models and remains far below the no-marker control on Qwen3-32B, isolating the operationalization axis from the boundary-extraction problem and confirming that the engineering bridge worth crossing is the automatic generation, validation, and enforcement of such typed structure from upstream interactions --- not the construction of richer prose summaries. A corrupted-allowlist stress test reinforces this interpretation: when the same typed boundary channel is misspelled, made noisy, or semantically inverted, leakage returns, showing that the bridge to deployment is not schema use alone but reliable boundary extraction and validation. We release the testbed, evaluator, and analysis scripts to support that work.

Three concrete next steps follow. First, the open engineering problem is \emph{automated typed-allowlist extraction}: producing, validating, and enforcing the \texttt{allowed\_audiences} schema directly from upstream interactions rather than from gold annotations, with paraphrase-robust validators that catch the category-level leaks exact-string redaction (E7) cannot. Second, the testbed is synthetic; the next empirical step is \emph{production-trace validation and user studies} that measure whether deployed agent handoffs show the same selective-fragility signature, and whether real stakeholders perceive the observed leaks as boundary violations. Third, all scenarios are English; \emph{multilingual replication} of the E8 dose-response gradient, particularly on languages with different modal verb systems such as German or Japanese, would test whether the L1$\to$L2 transition is language-universal or English-specific.
