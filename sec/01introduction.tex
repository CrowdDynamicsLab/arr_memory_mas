\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Multi-agent LLM handoffs systematically strip the boundary metadata that governs sensitive information while preserving the operational facts a downstream agent needs to act --- a structural privacy failure mode we call \emph{summary collapse}. The competence this failure mode breaks is everyday and well-formalized: when a person declines a meeting by saying ``I have a conflict at that time,'' they are sharing just enough---that the slot is unavailable---while withholding the rest: the therapy appointment, the job interview, the court hearing. This selective disclosure is not deception but a foundational social competence, formalized in communication theory under the framework of Communication Privacy Management (CPM)~\citep{petronio1991communication}. CPM describes how individuals maintain boundaries around private information, license its disclosure under specific audience and use conditions, and experience \emph{boundary turbulence} when those conditions are violated~\citep{petronio2002boundaries}. ``I have a conflict'' thins the boundary just enough to share the coordination-relevant fact while keeping the rest opaque.

Multi-agent LLM systems (MAS) are increasingly asked to perform this same act of selective disclosure on behalf of their users. As LLM agents are deployed for scheduling~\citep{geng2025realm}, care coordination~\citep{kim2026pair}, financial processing~\citep{xiao2024tradingagents}, and enterprise workflows~\citep{xiong2025self}, they handle information that is simultaneously useful for the task and sensitive in nature. The hard question is no longer whether a single agent can refrain from leaking a secret in its final output~\citep{10.1145/3687033}, but whether the \emph{system as a whole} preserves the right audience boundary as information flows through intermediate messages, summaries, and persisted memory~\citep{xu2025mem,li2026hippocampus}. A detail appropriate in a one-to-one exchange may become inappropriate the moment it is written into a handoff note and propagated to a wider set of agents.

A natural reading of recent results would frame this as either a model-norm problem or a memory-extraction problem. Privacy benchmarks for language-model agents largely measure whether models recognize contextual norms in a single decision~\citep{mireshghallah2023can,shao2024privacylens,zharmagambetov2025agentdam}, while a complementary line shows that sensitive content stored in agent memory or final outputs can be elicited by an adversary~\citep{yagoubi2026agentleak,wang-etal-2025-unveiling-privacy}. Adjacent security work establishes that inter-agent communication is itself a critical attack surface~\citep{he2025red,xie2026spark}. What none of these isolate is the structural step we focus on here: the moment an agent compresses upstream interaction into an artifact that a downstream agent will treat as ground truth. We call this the \emph{handoff summary}, and we argue that it is a systematic source of privacy leakage in MAS, distinct from norm misjudgment, memory extraction, and inter-agent injection.

Unlike benchmarks of single-agent norm judgment and adversarial extraction work, both of which treat one agent's decision as the unit of analysis, we isolate the cross-phase compression step itself as a structural source of boundary loss. Our central finding is that handoff summaries lose information in a structured way. They preserve the operational facts a downstream agent needs to act---times, entities, decisions, constraints---but are more likely to weaken or drop the \emph{boundary metadata} that governs how those facts may be used: audience constraints (``for the coordinator only''), ownership claims (``Alex told me this privately''), hedges (``probably,'' ``not confirmed''), and disclosure caveats (``do not include this in the final report''). We call this failure mode \emph{summary collapse} (\Cref{fig:summary_collapse}). Once the marker is stripped, a surviving fact appears to downstream agents as unconstrained shared state, and they propagate it accordingly.

We characterize summary collapse on a controlled multi-agent coordination testbed spanning seven domains, four handoff surfaces (explicit summarization, forwarding, memory replay, and report writing), and multiple handoff and downstream-pressure conditions. 
We introduce three operational measures: boundary-marker survival $\sigma$, the degree to which upstream boundary-metadata propositions persist into the handoff artifact; operational-fact survival $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$, the degree to which task-relevant operational facts persist; and compression ratio $\rho$, the proportional reduction in token length. Together, these measures separate how much information is lost from what kind.
We organize the empirical study around three research questions: \textbf{(RQ1)} does compression selectively reduce $\sigma$ while operational-fact survival remains stable? \textbf{(RQ2)} when sensitive context is forwarded, does the strength of the boundary marker causally determine whether it becomes audience-facing leakage? \textbf{(RQ3)} do lightweight mitigation strategies prevent leakage while preserving task utility?

Our key contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Summary collapse: identification and quantification.}
    We identify and quantify \emph{summary collapse}, a structural failure mode in multi-agent LLM coordination in which cross-phase compression preferentially strips boundary metadata while preserving the operational facts downstream agents act on. Unlike single-agent contextual-norm benchmarks~\citep{mireshghallah2023can,shao2024privacylens,zharmagambetov2025agentdam} and memory- or channel-extraction work~\citep{yagoubi2026agentleak,wang-etal-2025-unveiling-privacy,he2025red}, which treat one agent's decision or stored state as the unit of analysis, we measure the artifact produced \emph{between} agents. A judge-based marker-survival measure $\sigma$ and an operational-fact-survival measure $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$ (human-validated at $\kappa = 0.74$) decouple at the individual-handoff level on both primary models (Pearson $r$ near zero), and the gap widens under hard compression --- showing the loss is selective, not generic.
    \item \textbf{Marker strength, not presence, controls leakage.}
    Through matched-variant downstream tests we isolate marker \emph{strength}, not mere presence, as the determinant of audience-facing leakage. Unlike summarization-fidelity work that treats dropped hedges as a generic faithfulness issue~\citep{maynez2020faithfulness}, we connect marker explicitness to a downstream privacy outcome through a dose-response gradient: vague hedges like ``use discretion'' remain high-leakage ($73\%$ on GPT-5-mini and $50\%$ on DeepSeek in E8), while explicit constraints reduce leakage to under $15\%$ across all three tested models. A no-handoff single-agent control further shows that the failure is not reducible to multi-agent topology alone.
    \item \textbf{Operationalizing the boundary is the load-bearing axis.}
    We observe that prompt-only mitigations and deterministic phrase redaction each address only part of the failure --- redaction eliminates exact-string leakage but leaves paraphrase- and category-level leakage intact --- while a \emph{gold-derived} typed audience-allowlist probe drops leakage to $0/48$ on both primary models as an upper-bound existence point. Unlike adversarial inter-agent attack work~\citep{lee2024prompt,wang2025ip,he2025red} that frames mitigation as an extraction defense, we frame the load-bearing axis as \emph{operationalizing} the boundary into a machine-readable constraint that downstream agents act on rather than read as suggestion; whether an automated handoff can carry such typed structure remains the engineering bridge to future work.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Headline results.}
On the stratified $180$-handoff baseline, $\sigma$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$ decouple at the individual-handoff level (Pearson $r = -0.042$ on GPT-5-mini, $0.086$ on DeepSeek-R1-32B), and under $25$-word compression $\sigma$ falls to $\approx 0.6$ while $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$ stays at $\approx 0.98$ on all three models. In matched downstream tests, vague hedges like ``use discretion'' leak in $73\%$/$50\%$/$38\%$ of cases (GPT-5-mini/DeepSeek/Qwen3), while explicit constraints reduce leakage to under $15\%$ across all three models, and a gold-derived typed audience-allowlist probe drops it to $0/48$ on both primary models.
