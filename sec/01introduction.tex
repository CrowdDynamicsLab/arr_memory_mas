\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
When a person declines a meeting by saying ``I have a conflict at that time,'' they are sharing just enough---that the slot is unavailable---while withholding the rest: the therapy appointment, the job interview, the court hearing. This selective disclosure is not deception but a foundational social competence, formalized in communication theory under the framework of Communication Privacy Management (CPM)~\citep{petronio1991communication}. CPM describes how individuals maintain boundaries around private information, license its disclosure under specific audience and use conditions, and experience \emph{boundary turbulence} when those conditions are violated~\citep{petronio2002boundaries}. ``I have a conflict'' thins the boundary just enough to share the coordination-relevant fact while keeping the rest opaque.

Multi-agent LLM systems (MAS) are increasingly asked to perform this same act of selective disclosure on behalf of their users. As LLM agents are deployed for scheduling~\citep{geng2025realm}, care coordination~\citep{kim2026pair}, financial processing~\citep{xiao2024tradingagents}, and enterprise workflows~\citep{xiong2025self}, they handle information that is simultaneously useful for the task and sensitive in nature. The hard question is no longer whether a single agent can refrain from leaking a secret in its final output~\citep{10.1145/3687033}, but whether the \emph{system as a whole} preserves the right audience boundary as information flows through intermediate messages, summaries, and persisted memory~\citep{xu2025mem,li2026hippocampus}. A detail appropriate in a one-to-one exchange may become inappropriate the moment it is written into a handoff note and propagated to a wider set of agents.

A natural reading of recent results would frame this as either a model-norm problem or a memory-extraction problem. Privacy benchmarks for language-model agents largely measure whether models recognize contextual norms in a single decision~\citep{mireshghallah2023can,shao2024privacylens,zharmagambetov2025agentdam}, while a complementary line shows that sensitive content stored in agent memory or final outputs can be elicited by an adversary~\citep{yagoubi2026agentleak,wang-etal-2025-unveiling-privacy}. Adjacent security work establishes that inter-agent communication is itself a critical attack surface~\citep{he2025red,xie2026spark}. What none of these isolate is the structural step we focus on here: the moment an agent compresses upstream interaction into an artifact that a downstream agent will treat as ground truth. We call this the \emph{handoff summary}, and we argue that it is a systematic source of privacy leakage in MAS, distinct from norm misjudgment, memory extraction, and inter-agent injection.

Our central finding is that handoff summaries lose information in a structured way. They preserve the operational facts a downstream agent needs to act---times, entities, decisions, constraints---but are more likely to weaken or drop the \emph{boundary metadata} that governs how those facts may be used: audience constraints (``for the coordinator only''), ownership claims (``Alex told me this privately''), hedges (``probably,'' ``not confirmed''), and disclosure caveats (``do not include this in the final report''). We call this failure mode \emph{summary collapse} (\Cref{fig:summary_collapse}). Once the marker is stripped, a surviving fact appears to downstream agents as unconstrained shared state, and they propagate it accordingly.

We characterize summary collapse on a controlled multi-agent coordination testbed spanning seven domains, four handoff surfaces (explicit summarization, forwarding, memory replay, and report writing), and multiple handoff and downstream-pressure conditions. 
We introduce three operational measures: boundary-marker survival $\sigma$, the degree to which upstream boundary-metadata propositions persist into the handoff artifact; operational-fact survival $\sigma_{\mathrm{op}}$, the degree to which task-relevant operational facts persist; and compression ratio $\rho$, the proportional reduction in token length. Together, these measures separate how much information is lost from what kind.
We organize the empirical study around three research questions: \textbf{(RQ1)} does compression selectively reduce $\sigma$ while operational-fact survival remains stable? \textbf{(RQ2)} when sensitive context is forwarded, does the strength of the boundary marker causally determine whether it becomes audience-facing leakage? \textbf{(RQ3)} do lightweight mitigation strategies prevent leakage while preserving task utility?
